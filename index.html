1. Interview Strategy and Answering Framework

1.1 What the interview is likely evaluating

An interview covering EBX, Java, and Copilot may evaluate several dimensions:

  • Conceptual knowledge: Do you understand the fundamentals?
  • Technical depth: Can you explain how something works internally?
  • Practical experience: Have you implemented, debugged, or operated it?
  • Problem-solving: Can you reason through unfamiliar scenarios?
  • Communication: Can you explain a technical decision clearly?
  • Ownership: Do you understand the code and systems you deliver?
  • Learning agility: Can you work with unfamiliar tools and technologies?
  • Judgment: Do you know when to use a tool and when not to?

A good answer is not necessarily the longest answer. A strong technical answer usually has four parts:

  1. Definition: What is it?
  2. Mechanism: How does it work?
  3. Use case: Why/when would I use it?
  4. Trade-off: What are its limitations or alternatives?

Example: HashMap

Weak answer: “HashMap stores key-value pairs and is fast.”

Stronger answer: “HashMap stores key-value pairs and uses hashing to locate a bucket. It calculates a hash from the key, maps that hash to an internal bucket, and uses equality checks to identify the key. Average lookup is O(1), assuming a reasonable hash distribution, while collisions can increase the work. It is not thread-safe, and keys should have stable equals() and hashCode() behavior while they are in the map.”

This answer demonstrates definition, mechanism, complexity, and limitation.

1.2 Use the STAR method for experience questions

  • Situation: What was the context?
  • Task: What were you responsible for?
  • Action: What did you personally do?
  • Result: What changed or what was delivered?

Keep your answers truthful. Clearly distinguish what you personally implemented from what the team or another engineer implemented.

1.3 When you do not know an answer

Avoid bluffing. Use this pattern:

“I haven't implemented that directly, so I don't want to claim hands-on experience. My understanding is that it works this way… In a real implementation, I would first verify the version-specific behavior and then validate it with a small proof of concept.”

This shows honesty and engineering judgment.

1.4 How to handle follow-up questions

After your initial answer, expect:

  • Why?
  • How does it work internally?
  • What happens if it fails?
  • What are the alternatives?
  • What are the performance implications?
  • How would you test it?
  • What would you change at scale?

Study every concept at three depths:

  • Level 1: Explain it in one sentence.
  • Level 2: Explain how it works.
  • Level 3: Apply it to a realistic scenario and discuss trade-offs.

3. Phase 1 — MDM Fundamentals

3.1 What is Master Data Management?

Master Data Management (MDM) is the set of processes, governance practices, technology, and architecture used to create and maintain reliable, consistent, shared data about important business entities.

Examples of master data:

  • Customer
  • Product
  • Supplier
  • Employee
  • Location
  • Account
  • Asset

Master data is usually relatively stable compared with transactional data, but it can change over time.

Why MDM is needed

Without MDM, different systems may hold conflicting representations of the same entity.

Example:

System Customer Name Phone Address
CRM Acme Ltd. 111 Mumbai
Billing ACME Limited 222 Mumbai
ERP Acme 111 Pune

Questions arise:

  • Which record is correct?
  • Are these records the same customer?
  • Which source is trusted for each attribute?
  • Which systems should receive the corrected data?

MDM helps establish rules for identifying, reconciling, governing, and distributing trusted master data.

3.2 Master data vs. transactional data vs. reference data

Type Description Examples
Master data Core business entities Customer, Product, Supplier
Transactional data Events or business activities Order, Invoice, Payment
Reference data Controlled values used to classify data Country codes, currency codes, statuses

A customer is master data. An order placed by that customer is transactional data. A country code such as IN is reference data.

3.3 Golden Record

A Golden Record is the trusted, reconciled representation of an entity assembled from available records and governed by defined rules.

It is not necessarily a copy of one source record. Attributes may come from different sources.

Example:

  • CRM is authoritative for customer contact details.
  • ERP is authoritative for financial identifiers.
  • A compliance system is authoritative for compliance status.

The resulting Golden Record uses attribute-level trust or survivorship rules.

3.4 Single Source of Truth

A Single Source of Truth (SSOT) is the authoritative location or process for a particular data domain or attribute.

It does not always mean that every enterprise system reads from one physical database. In distributed architecture, the authoritative system may publish data to downstream systems.

3.5 Data governance

Data governance defines:

  • Data ownership
  • Roles and responsibilities
  • Standards
  • Policies
  • Quality rules
  • Access policies
  • Lifecycle rules
  • Stewardship processes

Governance answers: Who is allowed to create, change, approve, and trust this data?

3.6 Data stewardship

A data steward is responsible for the operational quality and governance of data within a domain.

Typical responsibilities:

  • Review data quality
  • Resolve duplicates
  • Approve changes
  • Maintain standards
  • Investigate exceptions
  • Coordinate with data owners

3.7 Data quality dimensions

Common dimensions:

  • Accuracy: Does data represent reality?
  • Completeness: Are required values present?
  • Consistency: Does data agree across systems?
  • Validity: Does data follow defined rules?
  • Uniqueness: Are duplicates controlled?
  • Timeliness: Is data current enough?
  • Integrity: Are relationships and constraints maintained?

Example

A product record:

  • Product code is missing → completeness problem.
  • Product code contains invalid characters → validity problem.
  • Product price differs between ERP and MDM → consistency problem.
  • Product is marked active despite being discontinued → accuracy/timeliness problem.
  • Two records represent the same product → uniqueness problem.

3.8 MDM implementation styles

Registry

The MDM system stores identifiers and links to source records, but may not hold all master attributes.

Pros: Lower data movement, relatively lightweight.
Cons: Consumers may still need to query source systems.

Consolidation

Data is collected into MDM to create a consolidated view. The MDM may not become the operational source for every update.

Pros: Strong visibility and analytics.
Cons: Source systems remain responsible for changes; synchronization can be complex.

Coexistence

Master data is managed across MDM and source systems, with bidirectional synchronization.

Pros: Supports existing operational systems.
Cons: Synchronization, conflict resolution, and ownership are complex.

Centralized

MDM becomes the primary system for creating and managing master data, distributing it to other systems.

Pros: Strong governance and central control.
Cons: Requires significant integration and organizational change.

Interview question: Which MDM style would you choose?

Model answer: “It depends on the existing system landscape, ownership model, operational requirements, and desired governance. If the organization needs a centralized process for creating and approving master data, centralized MDM may be appropriate. If legacy systems must continue managing data and MDM needs to reconcile and distribute changes, coexistence may be more realistic. I would evaluate source ownership, latency, integration complexity, and business readiness before choosing.”

4. Phase 1 — TIBCO EBX Architecture and Core Concepts

4.1 What is TIBCO EBX?

TIBCO EBX is a platform for managing governed enterprise data, including master data, reference data, data models, workflows, data quality, and integration with other systems.

Its value is not just storing records. It provides capabilities for:

  • Modeling data
  • Managing data lifecycle
  • Governance and stewardship
  • Validation
  • Workflow
  • Permissions
  • Data distribution and integration

4.2 Conceptual architecture

A simplified view:

Business Users / Data Stewards
              |
        EBX User Interface
              |
   Governance / Workflow / Services
              |
       Data Model & Repository
              |
     Data Spaces / Data Sets
              |
      Database / Persistence
              |
 APIs / Imports / Exports / Integrations
              |
      External Enterprise Systems

This is conceptual; the exact deployment architecture depends on the EBX version and implementation.

4.3 Important EBX terminology

Data model

Defines the structure, types, relationships, constraints, and behavior of data.

Data set

A logical container holding data conforming to a data model.

Data space

A logical area used to organize data and manage lifecycle or isolation. Exact usage depends on the implementation and EBX version.

Table

A collection of records of a defined structure.

Record

An individual row/entity instance in a table.

Primary key

An identifier used to uniquely identify a record within the defined key scope.

Foreign key / relationship

A reference connecting a record to another record.

Association

A relationship between data entities. It may be navigational and may have cardinality or constraints.

Validation

Rules that determine whether data is acceptable.

4.4 Why data spaces matter

Data spaces can support separation of data and lifecycle contexts. In implementations, they may be used for:

  • Development or working data
  • Reference data
  • Master data
  • Governance contexts
  • Data lifecycle management

Do not assume every project uses data spaces in the same way. Be prepared to explain the design in your actual implementation.

4.5 EBX model lifecycle

A typical conceptual lifecycle:

  1. Define the model.
  2. Configure tables and relationships.
  3. Define constraints and validation.
  4. Deploy or activate the model according to the environment's process.
  5. Load or create data.
  6. Validate data.
  7. Govern changes.
  8. Integrate with consuming systems.
  9. Evolve the model through controlled changes.

4.6 Interview questions

Q: What is EBX used for?
A: EBX is used to manage governed enterprise data. It supports data modeling, master and reference data management, validation, workflow, access control, and integration. It helps organizations maintain consistent and trusted data across systems.

Q: What is the difference between a data model and a data set?
A: A data model defines the structure and rules of data. A data set contains actual records that conform to that model.

Q: Why use EBX rather than a conventional database?
A: A database provides persistence and data access. EBX adds enterprise data management capabilities such as governance, workflow, validation, stewardship, model management, and user-facing data management. The choice depends on requirements; EBX is not automatically necessary for every application.

Q: What is the role of a data model?
A: It defines the structure, types, relationships, constraints, and business rules that govern data.

Q: What happens when a model changes?
A: The change must be assessed for compatibility with existing records, integrations, and consumers. A controlled deployment or migration may be required. The exact procedure is version and environment dependent.

5. Phase 1 — Data Modeling

5.1 Why data modeling matters

A good data model:

  • Represents the business domain accurately.
  • Enforces data integrity.
  • Supports governance.
  • Makes relationships clear.
  • Reduces duplication.
  • Supports future change.
  • Makes integrations easier.

A poor model can create complex workflows, difficult integrations, inconsistent data, and performance problems.

5.2 Entity, attribute, and relationship

  • Entity: A business object, such as Customer.
  • Attribute: A property, such as customer name.
  • Relationship: A connection between entities, such as Customer → Address.

Example:

Customer
--------
CustomerId
Name
Email
Status
Country

Address
-------
AddressId
CustomerId
AddressLine
City
PostalCode

5.3 Data types

Choose data types according to semantics, not convenience.

  • String: names, codes, descriptions
  • Integer: counts, numeric identifiers where appropriate
  • Decimal: monetary or precise numeric values
  • Boolean: true/false
  • Date: calendar date
  • Date-time: timestamp
  • Enumeration: controlled set of values
  • Reference: relationship to another record

Common mistake

Using a floating-point type for currency can introduce precision errors. Prefer decimal representations designed for exact decimal arithmetic.

5.4 Constraints

Constraints protect data integrity.

Examples:

  • Mandatory fields
  • Uniqueness
  • Range constraints
  • Pattern constraints
  • Referential integrity
  • Conditional rules

Example rule:

If CustomerStatus is Active, Email must be present and valid.

5.5 Relationship cardinality

  • One-to-one: One person has one passport record, in a simplified model.
  • One-to-many: One customer has many addresses.
  • Many-to-many: Products may belong to many categories; categories may contain many products.

A many-to-many relationship is often represented through an association table/entity.

5.6 Composition vs. association

Composition: The child is conceptually part of the parent and often has dependent lifecycle.

Association: Entities are related but can exist independently.

Example:

  • Order → Order Lines: often composition.
  • Customer → Product: association through orders or another relationship.

The exact semantics depend on business rules and implementation.

5.7 Normalization vs. denormalization

Normalization reduces redundancy and update anomalies by separating entities and relationships.

Denormalization duplicates or combines data to improve read performance or simplify consumers.

Trade-off:

  • Normalization: integrity, maintainability.
  • Denormalization: read performance, simpler access.
  • Denormalization requires synchronization and consistency controls.

5.8 Model evolution

When changing a model:

  1. Understand the business requirement.
  2. Identify affected records.
  3. Identify integrations and consumers.
  4. Assess backward compatibility.
  5. Plan migration or default values.
  6. Test with representative data.
  7. Deploy through controlled environments.
  8. Validate after deployment.

5.9 Interview questions

Q: How would you design a Product Master?

Model answer: “I would first identify the business domains and stakeholders. I would define a stable product identifier, core attributes, classifications, status, lifecycle dates, and relationships such as categories, brands, units, and suppliers. I would identify which attributes are mandatory, controlled, or source-owned. Then I would define validation, duplicate rules, governance workflows, access permissions, and downstream distribution requirements. I would also consider model extensibility and how product changes affect existing consumers.”

Q: How do you prevent invalid data?

Model answer: “I would enforce validation at multiple appropriate layers: model constraints for structural rules, business validation for domain rules, workflow checks for approval requirements, and integration validation for incoming data. I would return actionable error messages and avoid relying solely on UI validation.”

6.2 Matching and duplicate detection

Matching attempts to identify whether two records represent the same real-world entity.

Deterministic matching

Uses exact or rule-based comparison.

Example:

  • Same tax identifier
  • Same external customer ID

Probabilistic matching

Uses weighted similarity across attributes.

Example:

  • Name similarity
  • Address similarity
  • Phone similarity
  • Email similarity

A matching score might conceptually be:

Score =
  0.40 × NameSimilarity +
  0.25 × AddressSimilarity +
  0.20 × EmailSimilarity +
  0.15 × PhoneSimilarity

This is only illustrative. Real matching weights should be determined through domain analysis and testing.

6.3 Survivorship rules

Survivorship determines which values should be retained when records are consolidated.

Possible rules:

  • Source priority
  • Most recent update
  • Most complete value
  • Verified value
  • Attribute-specific authority
  • Manual steward decision

Avoid a universal “latest value wins” rule. The newest value may come from an unreliable source.

6.4 Data quality workflow

  1. Profile data.
  2. Identify defects.
  3. Define quality rules.
  4. Validate incoming records.
  5. Detect duplicates.
  6. Correct or enrich data.
  7. Assign exceptions to stewards.
  8. Monitor quality metrics.

6.5 Interview question: How do you improve data quality?

Model answer: “I would first profile the data to understand the defect patterns. Then I would define measurable quality rules for completeness, validity, uniqueness, consistency, and referential integrity. I would enforce preventive validation for new records and use remediation workflows for existing records. I would monitor metrics over time and assign ownership for exceptions.”

A workflow defines a sequence of tasks, decisions, and actions used to control a business process.

Example:

      ↓

      ↓

7.2 Workflow components

Depending on implementation:

13.8 Interview questions

Q: Is Java pass-by-value or pass-by-reference?
A: Java is pass-by-value. When an object reference is passed, the value of the reference is copied. Both variables may refer to the same object, so the object can be mutated, but reassigning the method parameter does not change the caller's reference.

Q: Can a constructor be overridden?
A: No. Constructors are not inherited and therefore cannot be overridden. They can be overloaded.

Q: Can static methods be overridden?
A: Static methods are hidden, not overridden in the polymorphic instance-method sense.

Q: What is the difference between == and equals()?
A: == compares primitive values or reference identity. equals() compares logical equality when implemented appropriately.

14. Phase 2 — OOP and SOLID

14.1 Encapsulation

Encapsulation hides internal state and exposes controlled operations.

class BankAccount {
    private BigDecimal balance;

    public BankAccount(BigDecimal initialBalance) {
        if (initialBalance.signum() < 0) {
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("Balance cannot be negative");
        }
        this.balance = initialBalance;
    }

    public BigDecimal getBalance() {
        return balance;
    }

    public void deposit(BigDecimal amount) {
        if (amount.signum() <= 0) {
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("Amount must be positive");
        }
        balance = balance.add(amount);
    }
}

14.2 Abstraction

Expose what an object can do while hiding implementation details.

interface PaymentService {
    void pay(BigDecimal amount);
}

The caller depends on the abstraction rather than a specific payment implementation.

14.3 Inheritance

Inheritance represents an “is-a” relationship and allows reuse or specialization.

Use it carefully. Deep inheritance hierarchies can be difficult to maintain.

14.4 Polymorphism

Polymorphism allows different implementations to be used through a common contract.

interface NotificationService {
    void send(String message);
}

class EmailNotification implements NotificationService {
    public void send(String message) {
        System.out.println("Email: " + message);
    }
}

class SmsNotification implements NotificationService {
    public void send(String message) {
        System.out.println("SMS: " + message);
    }
}

14.5 Overloading vs. overriding

Overloading

  • Same method name
  • Different parameter list
  • Usually resolved at compile time

Overriding

  • Subclass provides implementation of inherited instance method
  • Compatible signature and return type rules
  • Runtime polymorphism

14.6 Interface vs. abstract class

Use an interface to define a contract that multiple types can implement.

Use an abstract class when related classes share state or implementation and a common base is meaningful.

Interfaces can have default, static, and private methods depending on Java version. Abstract classes can have constructors and instance state.

14.7 Composition vs. inheritance

Composition means an object contains or delegates to another object.

Prefer composition when:

  • Behavior should be replaceable.
  • The relationship is “has-a.”
  • You want less coupling to an inheritance hierarchy.

14.8 SOLID

S — Single Responsibility Principle

A class should have one primary responsibility or reason to change.

O — Open/Closed Principle

Software should be open to extension but closed to unnecessary modification.

L — Liskov Substitution Principle

Subtypes should be usable wherever their base type is expected without violating expected behavior.

I — Interface Segregation Principle

Clients should not depend on interfaces containing methods they do not need.

D — Dependency Inversion Principle

High-level modules should depend on abstractions rather than concrete low-level details.

15.1 Collection selection

15.2 ArrayList

  • Backed by a resizable array.
  • get(index) is O(1).
  • Appending is amortized O(1).
  • Inserting/removing in the middle is O(n) due to shifting.
  • Capacity growth may copy elements.

15.3 LinkedList

  • Nodes linked together.
  • Indexed access is O(n).
  • Insert/remove can be O(1) when the node position is already known.
  • Locating the position is often O(n).
  • Higher memory overhead than array-based structures.

15.4 HashMap

Conceptually:

  1. Calculate key hash.
  2. Transform/map hash to a bucket.
  3. Find entries in the bucket.
  4. Compare keys using equality.
  5. Return or update value.

Average lookup/insertion is commonly O(1), but worst-case behavior depends on collisions and implementation details.

Modern Java HashMap implementations may treeify heavily collided buckets under certain conditions. Exact thresholds and details are implementation-specific; know the principle rather than relying on memorized constants unless asked about a specific JDK.

15.5 equals() and hashCode()

Contract:

  • If two objects are equal according to equals(), they must have the same hash code.
  • Unequal objects may share a hash code.
  • equals() should be reflexive, symmetric, transitive, consistent, and return false for null.

Example:

class Employee {
    private final int id;

    Employee(int id) {
        this.id = id;
    }

    @Override
    public boolean equals(Object obj) {
        if (this == obj) return true;
        if (!(obj instanceof Employee other)) return false;
        return id == other.id;
    }

    @Override
    public int hashCode() {
        return Integer.hashCode(id);
    }
}

15.6 Why mutable keys are dangerous

If a key's hash-related fields change after insertion, the map may not find it in the expected bucket.

Prefer immutable keys or fields that do not change while the key is in a map/set.

15.7 HashSet

A HashSet is typically backed by a HashMap and stores elements as keys.

It provides uniqueness based on equals() and hashCode().

15.8 Comparable vs. Comparator

Comparable: Defines natural ordering inside the class.

Comparator: Defines external or alternative ordering.

employees.sort(
    Comparator.comparing(Employee::getLastName)
              .thenComparing(Employee::getFirstName)
);

15.9 ConcurrentHashMap

Provides concurrent access designed for multi-threaded use. It does not permit null keys or values in standard Java implementations.

Use it when multiple threads need safe concurrent access to a map. It does not make compound business operations automatically atomic; use appropriate atomic methods or synchronization.

15.10 Interview questions

Q: Why is HashMap not thread-safe?
A: Its operations are not designed to provide thread-safe coordination for concurrent modifications. Concurrent access can produce inconsistent behavior or lost updates. Use ConcurrentHashMap or external synchronization when required.

Q: HashMap vs. Hashtable?
A: Hashtable is a legacy synchronized map and disallows null keys and values. HashMap is generally preferred for non-concurrent use. For concurrent use, ConcurrentHashMap is usually a better choice than Hashtable.

Q: HashMap vs. TreeMap?
A: HashMap provides average constant-time lookup without sorted ordering. TreeMap maintains sorted keys and typically provides logarithmic operations.

Q: What happens if two keys have the same hash?
A: They collide and are stored in the same bucket structure. Equality is then used to distinguish keys.

18. Phase 2 — Concurrency

18.1 Process vs. thread

A process is an executing program with resources and memory space. Threads are execution paths within a process and generally share process memory.

18.2 Runnable vs. Callable

  • Runnable: no result, cannot declare checked exceptions in its method signature.
  • Callable: returns a result and can throw checked exceptions.

18.3 ExecutorService

Prefer managed thread pools over manually creating threads for many application tasks.

ExecutorService executor = Executors.newFixedThreadPool(4);

try {
    Future<Integer> result = executor.submit(() -> 2 + 2);
    System.out.println(result.get());
} finally {
    executor.shutdown();
}

In production, pool size and lifecycle should be configured according to workload and application needs.

18.4 Race condition

A race condition occurs when behavior depends on timing or interleaving of concurrent operations.

Example: two threads increment a shared counter. A read-modify-write operation is not automatically atomic.

18.5 Synchronization

Synchronization protects critical sections and coordinates visibility and ordering.

class Counter {
    private int count;

    public synchronized void increment() {
        count++;
    }

    public synchronized int getCount() {
        return count;
    }
}

18.6 Volatile

volatile provides visibility and ordering guarantees for a variable. It does not make compound operations such as count++ atomic.

18.7 Atomic classes

Atomic classes provide atomic operations for supported values and operations.

AtomicInteger count = new AtomicInteger();
count.incrementAndGet();

18.8 Deadlock

A deadlock occurs when threads wait indefinitely for resources held by each other.

Prevention techniques:

  • Consistent lock ordering
  • Minimize nested locks
  • Use timeouts where appropriate
  • Avoid unnecessary shared state
  • Inspect thread dumps

18.9 CompletableFuture

Useful for composing asynchronous operations.

CompletableFuture<String> future =
    CompletableFuture.supplyAsync(() -> loadData())
        .thenApply(data -> transform(data))
        .exceptionally(ex -> "Fallback");

Know:

  • Async execution
  • Composition
  • Exception handling
  • Executor choice
  • Avoiding blocking chains

18.10 Interview questions

Q: What is the difference between synchronized and volatile?
A: Synchronization provides mutual exclusion and visibility guarantees around a critical section. Volatile provides visibility and ordering for a variable but does not provide mutual exclusion or make compound operations atomic.

Q: How would you make a service thread-safe?
A: Identify mutable shared state, minimize it, use immutable objects where possible, choose concurrent structures or synchronization for shared state, and test concurrent behavior.

Q: What is a deadlock?
A: Threads are blocked waiting for locks/resources in a cycle, so none can proceed.

21.1 Find duplicates

List<Integer> numbers = List.of(1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5);

Set<Integer> seen = new HashSet<>();
Set<Integer> duplicates = new HashSet<>();

for (Integer number : numbers) {
    if (!seen.add(number)) {
        duplicates.add(number);
    }
}

Complexity: Average O(n) time and O(n) space.

Optional<Integer> secondHighest =
    numbers.stream()
        .distinct()
        .sorted(Comparator.reverseOrder())
        .skip(1)
        .findFirst();

  • @Controller
  • @Bean

  • Prototype

  • Session

  • Operational features

22.7 Auto-configuration

24. Phase 3 — Persistence, SQL, and Transactions

24.1 SQL fundamentals

24.2 Joins

  • INNER JOIN: matching rows
  • LEFT JOIN: all left rows plus matching right rows
  • RIGHT JOIN: all right rows plus matching left rows
  • FULL JOIN: all rows from both sides, matched where possible

24.3 Indexes

Indexes can improve retrieval but add storage and write overhead.

Use indexes based on:

  • Query patterns
  • Selectivity
  • Join keys
  • Filtering and ordering
  • Data volume

24.4 ACID

  • Atomicity: all or nothing
  • Consistency: valid state transitions
  • Isolation: concurrent transactions do not improperly interfere
  • Durability: committed changes persist

24.5 Transaction boundaries

A transaction should cover the business operation that needs consistency.

Avoid:

  • Transactions that are unnecessarily long
  • External network calls inside transactions where possible
  • Unclear rollback behavior
  • Multiple independent operations without failure handling

24.6 JPA and Hibernate

JPA is a persistence specification; Hibernate is a common implementation.

Know:

  • Entity
  • Persistence context
  • Entity lifecycle
  • Dirty checking
  • Flush
  • Lazy loading
  • Eager loading
  • Cascading
  • Relationships

24.7 N+1 query problem

A query retrieves N parent records and then triggers one additional query per parent for related data.

Possible remedies:

  • Fetch joins
  • Entity graphs
  • Batch fetching
  • DTO projections
  • Query redesign

Choose based on the data and use case. Fetching everything eagerly can create different performance problems.

24.8 Optimistic vs. pessimistic locking

Optimistic locking: Detects conflicts, commonly using a version field.

Pessimistic locking: Locks resources to prevent competing operations.

Use optimistic locking where conflicts are relatively uncommon. Use pessimistic locking where contention and correctness requirements justify it.

27. Phase 4 — AI and LLM Fundamentals

27.1 AI, ML, DL, and Generative AI

  • AI: Broad field of systems performing tasks associated with intelligence.
  • ML: Systems learn patterns from data.
  • DL: Machine learning using multi-layer neural networks.
  • Generative AI: Models generate content such as text, code, images, or audio.

27.2 Large Language Models

LLMs are trained to model language and generate sequences of tokens. They can produce useful explanations and code but may also produce incorrect or fabricated information.

27.3 Tokens

Tokens are units used by models to process text. They may represent words, word pieces, punctuation, or other units.

27.4 Context window

The context window is the amount of information the model can process for a particular interaction. Too much irrelevant context can reduce the usefulness of a prompt.

27.5 Training vs. inference

  • Training: model parameters are learned or adjusted from data.
  • Inference: the trained model generates or predicts outputs.

27.6 Hallucination

A hallucination is output that appears plausible but is unsupported or incorrect.

For coding:

  • A method may not exist.
  • A library version may be wrong.
  • A generated implementation may be insecure.
  • The code may compile but not satisfy the requirement.

27.7 Embeddings

Embeddings represent content as numerical vectors, allowing similarity comparisons and retrieval based on semantic relationships.

Common use cases:

  • Semantic search
  • Recommendation
  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
  • Clustering

27.8 Transformer and attention — interview-level explanation

Transformers process token representations using attention mechanisms that allow the model to relate different parts of the input.

Attention helps the model determine which contextual elements are relevant when producing representations and predictions.

You should be able to explain the idea without claiming to implement transformer internals unless you have done so.

Closing Study Principle

Do not try to memorize the entire study bible in one pass.

For each module:

  1. Understand the concept.
  2. Explain it without notes.
  3. Write or run a practical example.
  4. Answer interview questions.
  5. Identify gaps.
  6. Revise those gaps.
  7. Move forward when you can explain and apply the topic.

The goal is to become a confident engineer who can explain fundamentals, demonstrate practical experience, reason through unfamiliar problems, and take ownership of solutions.
